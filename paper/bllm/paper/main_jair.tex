\begin{abstract}
{\bf Background:}
A system that improves itself keeps every change that passes a check, so a check that makes errors lets mistakes pile up. How much checking keeps such a system sound has not been computed.

{\bf Objectives:}
We compute the least checking that keeps a self-improving system sound, the least number of guesses a proposer needs before a right one appears, and the cost of stacking checked layers, and we build an engine on these laws.

{\bf Methods:}
We prove the bounds in a model of repeated random checks, check their finite inequalities in Lean~4, and build Anaxia, an engine that combines human constructions, a shared memory, a mode that builds new objects together with the argument for what they force, stacked layers, and a loop that changes one of its own rules after each attempt that fails.

{\bf Results:}
The least checking is an entropy of the costs of the changes, and an endless chain of improvements stays sound at finite cost exactly when that entropy is finite. A stack of checked layers costs more than its layers would cost alone. Human authors come close to the least number of guesses, and nine language models need $1.5$ to $4.3$ times as many. Memory halves the checks the engine needs. From the rules of a colouring problem alone, the engine rebuilt the published 2016 counterexample to Steinberg's conjecture. It proved a conjecture on the four-dimensional reflexive polytopes behind Calabi--Yau threefolds: shrunk one reflexive polytope at a time, each of them reaches a seed in at most $\seedBound$ steps.

{\bf Conclusions:}
The price of soundness is an entropy, and it can be computed. The engine is a work in progress, and its rules and search are being revised and extended for the open problems it has not yet settled.
\end{abstract}
